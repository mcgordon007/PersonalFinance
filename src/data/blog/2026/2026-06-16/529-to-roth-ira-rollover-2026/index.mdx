## The Rule, Plain

Starting January 1, 2024, a 529 account holder can roll over up to $35,000 in a lifetime to a Roth IRA opened by the 529 beneficiary. The rollover is not subject to federal income tax or the 10% penalty that normally applies to non-qualified 529 withdrawals. The annual rollover amount cannot exceed the annual Roth IRA contribution limit for 2025 and 2026, that limit is $7,000 for individuals under age 50, and $8,000 for those 50 and older. So the $35,000 lifetime cap is built up over multiple years of rollovers. Three other requirements matter: the 529 account must have been open for at least 15 years; contributions made within the last five years (and the earnings on those contributions) are not eligible; and the Roth IRA receiving the funds must be in the name of the 529 beneficiary.

## Why It Matters

Before SECURE 2.0, the choice for an overfunded 529 was one of four options, all of which had downsides. Change the beneficiary to another family member, spend it on K-12 tuition (capped at $10,000 per year), take the penalty hit on non-qualified withdrawals, or leave it sitting indefinitely. The Roth rollover changes the math entirely. The same money that was trapped in a college-only account can now seed a retirement account with the same tax-free growth and tax-free withdrawals that make Roth IRAs one of the most powerful long-term savings vehicles. Consider an example: a grandparent opened a 529 in 2009 for a grandchild now 22 and working. The 529 has $35,000 in it, all contributions made more than five years ago. The grandchild earns $40,000 well under the Roth IRA income limit. They can roll $7,000 per year from the 529 into their Roth IRA for five years, totaling $35,000. With assumed 7% annual growth over 40 years until age 62, that $35,000 grows to approximately $525,000 entirely tax-free.

## The 15-Year Rule and the Five-Year Look-Back

The 15-year account age requirement is the most important constraint and the one families most often miss. If you opened a 529 in 2010 for a baby, the account is not eligible for a Roth rollover until 2025. If you opened a 529 in 2018 for a teenager about to start college, the account will not be eligible until 2033. This matters for planning. A parent opening a 529 today for a newborn will not be able to use the rollover until 2040. The five-year look-back on contributions is designed to prevent abuse: you cannot dump $35,000 into a 529 this year and roll it to a Roth next January. Contributions made within the last five years are not eligible to be rolled over, and the earnings on those recent contributions are excluded. The IRS treats the oldest contributions and their earnings as rolling over first.

## How to Actually Do the Rollover

The mechanics are similar to a 401(k)-to-IRA rollover. The 529 plan administrator initiates a distribution directly to a Roth IRA in the beneficiary's name. The receiving Roth IRA custodian receives the funds as a direct contribution. You do not touch the money. The 529 plan sends a Form 1099-Q showing the distribution, and the Roth IRA custodian sends a Form 5498 showing the rollover contribution. You report both on your tax return, but the rollover itself is not taxable. The key is making sure both forms match in amount and beneficiary name.

## The Bottom Line

The 529-to-Roth IRA rollover is one of the most significant wealth transfer tools added to the tax code in decades. It solves the overfunding problem that kept many families from maximizing 529 contributions. It turns unused education savings into retirement wealth with the same tax advantages that make Roth IRAs so powerful. For families with old 529 accounts, the rollover window is open now. For new parents, the message is simple: open the 529 early, fund it consistently, and let the 15-year clock start. The compounding math favors the patient, and this rule rewards families who plan ahead.
